Pairing Proofs and Polynomial Ring Toolkit

polyring verifies a Groth16 proof inside another Groth16 proof, the building block of recursive SNARKs, and then on-chain. It checks each Miller loop step as one polynomial identity instead of a chain of 𝔽p12 multiplications, so the recursive verifier needs 38% fewer constraints than gnark's own pairing and the outer proof is cheaper to produce.

Shramee Srivastav, MIST.cash

Impact

The paper's saving holds in the real implementation

The paper measured a 3-pairing product check on a gnark fork. The library runs the same technique on patched gnark, inside a complete recursive Groth16 verifier. Across both, and in production on Starknet, polyring removes about a third of the cost of a pairing check.

Recursive proofs

Verify a Groth16 proof inside your circuit

A recursive SNARK checks one proof inside another. In a Groth16 verifier circuit that check is dominated by the pairing: with gnark's pairing, the Miller loop and final exponentiation are 83% of the constraints for a BN254 inner proof. polyring rewrites exactly that part, so every recursive step is cheaper to prove, and the result is still one ordinary Groth16 proof that a chain can verify.

  1. 1 · Inner

    Your statement

    Any gnark circuit, compiled over BN254 or BLS12-381, proved with stock Groth16.

  2. 2 · Inner proof

    π and its key

    The proof, its public inputs and the verifying key, which becomes circuit constants.

  3. 3 · Outer, BN254

    Ring pairing

    recursion.NewVerifier checks π inside your circuit, next to your own constraints.

  4. 4 · Outer proof

    One proof, 512 bytes

    A BN254 Groth16 proof with three BSB22 commitments, proved with stock gnark.

  5. 5 · On-chain

    Solidity verifier

    lib/solidity generates the contract; verifyProof costs about 460k gas.

Inner curve

Wrap and aggregate

Fold a proof, plus whatever you check about it, into one BN254 Groth16 proof. The outer prover does 38% less work than with gnark's pairing for the same statement.

Switch curves for Ethereum

Verify a BLS12-381 Groth16 proof inside a BN254 circuit. The outer proof is checked by the BN254 pairing precompile (EIP-197) that Ethereum and most EVM chains provide.

Any pairing check

The ring pairing's PairingCheck is not tied to Groth16. A bare two-pair check with both G2 points from the witness, the most general case, costs 30.7% (BN254) and 21.5% (BLS12-381) fewer constraints.

On-chain verification

Cheaper pairing checks on-chain, and what they cost on the EVM

How much polyring saves on chain depends on who computes the pairing. Where a VM executes it step by step, the technique makes verification itself cheaper. Where a precompile computes it at a fixed price, the saving moves to the prover.

Starknet, CairoVM, zkVMs

When the VM computes the pairing

Starknet has no pairing precompile: the CairoVM runs the pairing check, metered by steps, and the prover's hints arrive as calldata. Garaga verifies Groth16 proofs with this paper's consolidated relations. They cut VM steps by 37.1% and committed elements by 50.7% for BN254, which fit a Groth16 verification within Starknet's transaction limits for the first time.

Fewer committed elements means less to hash and less hint calldata per verification. The paper names gnark's ecpair precompile circuit, which zkEVMs use to prove the EVM's pairing precompile, as the next place the toolkit applies.

Ethereum and EVM chains

When a precompile computes the pairing

On the EVM the BN254 pairing runs in a precompile at a fixed price, so no circuit technique makes that call cheaper. What polyring changes is what you can verify for that price: a BN254 or BLS12-381 proof, plus any logic you add, becomes one BN254 Groth16 proof, and lib/solidity generates the contract that checks it.

The ring checks draw three BSB22 commitments instead of gnark's one, and the contract checks each of them. That is the trade: a cheaper prover for a more expensive verifier.

  • polyring pays off when proving dominates: you produce many outer proofs, prove on limited hardware, or pay a proving service by the constraint.
  • gnark's pairing pays off when one proof is verified many times on chain.
Step-by-step guide

Add a Groth16 verifier circuit inside your circuit

Eight steps from an inner proof to a contract that verifies the recursive proof. Every code block below is taken from a module that runs the whole flow, inner proof to on-chain verification, in about a minute on an Apple M3 Pro. Requirements: Go 1.26.8, and solc on your PATH for step 7.

Only wrapping a proof, with no logic of your own? Skip step 4: recursion.NewBN254Circuit(vk) (or NewBLS12381Circuit) is a complete outer circuit, and its Assign(proof, public) method fills the witness.

  1. Add polyring and its gnark pin

    polyring needs a gnark builder fix that is not upstream yet. Go ignores replace directives in dependencies, so your module has to repeat polyring's.

    shell
    go get github.com/mistcash/polyring
    go.mod
    // Copy this line from polyring's go.mod; it moves with polyring.
replace github.com/consensys/gnark v0.16.4 => github.com/mistcash/tiny-gnark v0.0.0-20261018205550-0a3abd2ee477

    Then run go mod tidy. Check polyring's go.mod for the current line before you copy it.

  2. Prove your inner statement

    Any gnark circuit over BN254 or BLS12-381, proved with stock Groth16. You need three things from it: the verifying key, the proof and the public witness.

    inner.go
    // Error handling omitted. For a BLS12-381 inner proof use ecc.BLS12_381.
ccs, err := frontend.Compile(ecc.BN254.ScalarField(), r1cs.NewBuilder, &InnerCircuit{})
pk, innerVK, err := groth16.Setup(ccs)
full, err := frontend.NewWitness(innerAssignment, ecc.BN254.ScalarField())
innerProof, err := groth16.Prove(ccs, pk, full)
innerPublic, err := full.Public()

    To try the flow first, mimc.Prove(ecc.BN254) from polyring's examples does all of this for a MiMC preimage circuit.

    No BSB22 commitments in the inner circuit. It must not call api.Commit, directly or through a gnark gadget that commits internally (range checks, lookup tables and emulated arithmetic can). Step 3 returns an error if the key has commitments.

  3. Turn the inner verifying key into circuit constants

    The inner key is baked into the outer circuit. NewVerifyingKey checks that α, β, γ and δ are valid subgroup points, precomputes e(α, β) and the line evaluations for γ and δ, and rejects keys with commitments.

    outer.go
    vk, err := recursion.NewVerifyingKey(innerVK)

  4. Put the verifier inside your circuit

    Add the inner proof and its public inputs as fields, keep the key in an unexported field, and verify the proof as the first thing in Define. Your own constraints follow.

    outer.go
    // The inner proof's curve. For BLS12-381 inner proofs use
// emparams.BLS12381Fp / BLS12381Fr and sw_bls12381.G2Affine / GTEl.
type (
	Fp   = emparams.BN254Fp // inner curve base field
	Fr   = emparams.BN254Fr // inner curve scalar field
	G2   = sw_bn254.G2Affine
	GTEl = sw_bn254.GTEl
)

// Outer is your own circuit. It verifies one inner Groth16 proof, then
// adds constraints of its own.
type Outer struct {
	InnerProof  recursion.Proof[Fp, G2]     `gnark:",secret"`
	InnerPublic recursion.PublicWitness[Fr] `gnark:",public"`
	Nonce       frontend.Variable           `gnark:",public"` // your own inputs

	vk *recursion.VerifyingKey // the inner key, baked in as constants
}

// NewOuter returns the circuit with the inner key set and the inner public
// inputs sized. Use it for both compiling and assigning.
func NewOuter(vk *recursion.VerifyingKey) *Outer {
	return &Outer{InnerPublic: recursion.NewBN254Circuit(vk).PublicWitness, vk: vk}
}

func (c *Outer) Define(api frontend.API) error {
	// First line of Define: the ring checker must exist before anything
	// else in the circuit creates an emulated field.
	v, err := recursion.NewVerifier[Fp, Fr, G2, GTEl](api, c.vk)
	if err != nil {
		return err
	}
	if err := v.AssertProof(c.InnerProof, c.InnerPublic); err != nil {
		return err
	}
	// Your own constraints go here.
	api.AssertIsDifferent(c.Nonce, 0)
	return nil
}

    Why first: the ring checks emit range checks of their own, and gnark runs deferred checks in registration order, so the ring checker has to be registered before any other emulated field.

  5. Compile the outer circuit and run setup

    The outer circuit is always over BN254, whichever curve the inner proof uses. Compile with the same vk you will assign with.

    main.go
    ccs, err := frontend.Compile(ecc.BN254.ScalarField(), r1cs.NewBuilder, NewOuter(vk))
pk, outerVK, err := groth16.Setup(ccs) // about 491k constraints

    groth16.Setup is a local trusted setup, fine for tests. Keys that secure anything need a setup ceremony.

  6. Assign the witness and prove

    The outer circuit helper converts the native proof and public witness into circuit values; copy them into your assignment.

    main.go
    tmp := recursion.NewBN254Circuit(vk)
if err := tmp.Assign(innerProof, innerPublic); err != nil {
	return err
}
assignment := NewOuter(vk)
assignment.InnerProof, assignment.InnerPublic = tmp.Proof, tmp.PublicWitness
assignment.Nonce = 7 // your own inputs

full, err := frontend.NewWitness(assignment, ecc.BN254.ScalarField())
public, err := full.Public()
proof, err := groth16.Prove(ccs, pk, full,
	gnarksolidity.WithProverTargetSolidityVerifier(backend.GROTH16))
err = groth16.Verify(proof, outerVK, public,
	gnarksolidity.WithVerifierTargetSolidityVerifier(backend.GROTH16))

    The Solidity target makes the prover derive its commitment challenges with Keccak, as the contract does. Leave it out if the proof is only verified off chain.

  7. Export the Solidity verifier and call it

    Use polyring's exporter: gnark's own generator supports one commitment, and polyring proofs carry three.

    main.go
    bnVK := outerVK.(*groth16bn254.VerifyingKey)
bnProof := proof.(*groth16bn254.Proof)

f, err := os.Create("Verifier.sol")
err = solidity.ExportSolidity(bnVK, f)          // polyring's lib/solidity
proofBytes := solidity.MarshalSolidity(bnProof) // 512 bytes

// Public inputs: the outer public witness, minus the values the
// contract derives from the commitments itself.
vec := public.Vector().(fr.Vector)
n := len(bnVK.G1.K) - 1 - len(bnVK.PublicAndCommitmentCommitted)
inputs := make([]*big.Int, n)
for i := range inputs {
	inputs[i] = vec[i].BigInt(new(big.Int))
}
// On chain: Verifier.verifyProof(proofBytes, inputs) reverts unless the proof is valid.

    Each inner public input enters as four 64-bit limbs, so this example, with one inner public input and a nonce, passes five inputs.

  8. Test it

    Check the assignment against the circuit before paying for setup, then run polyring's own end-to-end tests as a reference.

    outer_test.go
    // Seconds, no setup: does the assignment satisfy the circuit?
err := test.IsSolved(NewOuter(vk), assignment, ecc.BN254.ScalarField())
    shell, in a polyring checkout
    go test ./examples/recursion/ -run TestGroth16Verifier -v   # both inner curves, tampered proofs rejected
go test ./examples/recursion/ -run TestOuterOnChain -v      # prove, deploy, verify on a simulated EVM (needs solc)

How it works

One polynomial identity per Miller loop step

A pairing check is a Miller loop over 𝔽p12 followed by a final exponentiation. Earlier work checks every 𝔽p12 multiplication on its own, as a polynomial identity A·B = Q·P12 + R tested at a random point, and commits every intermediate remainder. The paper checks a whole Miller loop step at once, so the intermediate products are never committed:

F(x)2 · L1(x) · L2(x) · L3(x) = Q(x) · P12(x) + R(x)

F is the Miller loop accumulator, L1 to L3 are the sparse line functions of three pairs, and P12 is the modulus of the extension, x12 − 18x6 + 82 for BN254. All step identities, the final exponentiation's residue-witness check included, are batched with a random linear combination into one check at a random point. The circuit pays for one evaluation of each polynomial and a single equality, and the soundness error stays below 2−244 for BN254 after Fiat–Shamir.

A polynomial ring toolkit for gnark

The protocol is packaged as a reusable toolkit for any emulated field and any monic modulus, not just pairings: register a ring, submit products, and the checker proves them all with two commitments when Define returns. The paper names lattice schemes such as ML-KEM and ML-DSA, which work in rings like ℤq[x]/(xn + 1), as a next application.

Builds on Youssef El Housni's Pairings in Rank-1 Constraint Systems, Feltroidprime's polynomial identity testing for 𝔽p12, and Novakovic and Eagen's On Proving Pairings, whose residue witness replaces the final exponentiation.

std/polyring
prc := polyring.NewPolyRingChecker[emulated.BN254Fp](api) // before any other emulated field
modPoly := prc.MakePoly(82, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, -18, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1)
ring := prc.NewPolyRingCheck(modPoly, nil)
res, _ := prc.MulPolyRings(ring, A, B, C) // (A·B·C) mod modPoly, checked when Define returns
acc := prc.NewPolyRingAccumulator(ring, targetDeg)
acc.Mul(A).Mul(B).Sqr() // enqueue (A·B)², nothing multiplied yet

Scope

What polyring does not do yet

v0.1 verifies one Groth16 proof of one fixed inner circuit inside a BN254 Groth16 circuit. These are scope decisions for the first release, each with a reason.

No nested recursion

An outer proof carries three BSB22 commitments, and the verifier does not check inner commitments, so an outer proof cannot itself be the inner proof. Supporting it means verifying commitments and their proof of knowledge in-circuit.

A fixed verifying key

The inner key is a compile-time constant. That is what lets γ and δ use precomputed lines and e(α, β) fold into a constant; a runtime key would give those savings back.

One proof per outer proof

Aggregating several inner proofs with a shared Miller loop would save more per proof. It is not implemented yet.

Unaudited

A bug in an in-circuit pairing is a soundness break, not a crash. Use polyring for research and on testnets until it has been audited.

FAQ

Questions

How do I verify a Groth16 proof inside a gnark circuit?

Add polyring and its gnark replace directive to go.mod, turn the inner verifying key into constants with recursion.NewVerifyingKey, call recursion.NewVerifier as the first statement of your circuit's Define, then AssertProof on the inner proof and public inputs. The outer circuit compiles over BN254 and proves with stock gnark Groth16. The step-by-step guide has the tested code.

Does polyring make on-chain verification cheaper?

Where the chain computes the pairing in its own VM, yes: the technique cut CairoVM steps by 37.1% for a BN254 Groth16 pairing check in Garaga on Starknet. On the EVM the pairing runs in a precompile, so polyring's outer verifier costs more gas than gnark's (about 460k against 359k, +28%); the saving there is on the prover, 38% fewer constraints per recursive proof.

Can I verify a BLS12-381 Groth16 proof on Ethereum with polyring?

Yes, through recursion. polyring verifies the BLS12-381 proof inside a BN254 Groth16 circuit, and the resulting BN254 proof is checked on-chain by the generated Solidity verifier through the EVM's BN254 pairing precompile.

How is this different from gnark's own recursive Groth16 verifier?

gnark's verifier checks the pairing with gnark's emulated pairing over 𝔽p12. polyring's baseline is the same outer circuit with that pairing; only the pairing check differs. The ring pairing costs 38.0% fewer constraints for a BN254 inner proof and 30.9% for BLS12-381, and draws two more BSB22 commitments, which is why polyring ships its own Solidity exporter.

Why do the paper's numbers differ from the library's?

The paper measured a bare 3-pairing check on a gnark fork that predates two gnark soundness fixes. The fixes add range checks to every emulated multiplication, which grows gnark's pairing and polyring's alike, and the library measures a complete Groth16 verifier, not just the pairings. The relative saving holds: 35.7% in the paper, 38.6% on the same circuit after the fixes (preliminary), 38.0% for the full verifier. ARTIFACT.md maps each number to its source.

Is polyring audited?

No. polyring is unaudited research software at v0.1. Use it on testnets and for research, not to secure funds.

The paper

Pairing Proofs and Polynomial Ring Toolkit

Shramee Srivastav, MIST.cash. IACR Transactions on Cryptographic Hardware and Embedded Systems (TCHES), 2027, to appear. Open access under CC BY 4.0.

Abstract

Elliptic curve pairings are fundamental to modern zero-knowledge proof systems, including Groth16, PLONK, and KZG polynomial commitments. In resource-constrained execution environments (Ethereum VM, BitVM, blockchain smart contracts), where computation is metered by gas fees, and in arithmetized computation systems (R1CS, Plonkish, Cairo AIR), where each computational step imposes significant prover overhead, the cost of pairing computation remains a practical bottleneck. We present a public-coin interactive proof system that consolidates the Miller loop iterations and final exponentiation into a single polynomial relation. The relation can then be verified as polynomial identities, greatly reducing the pairing costs. We present a full implementation in gnark (the industry-standard framework for arithmetized circuit development) as a reusable polynomial ring toolkit in the emulated package. Benchmarked on a 3-pairing product check (the pairing workload of a Groth16 verification), our gnark implementation achieves over 32% reduction in constraints, over 50% reduction in committed elements, and reductions in both compile-time and solver memory for both BN254 and BLS12-381 curves. This enables practical pairing verification in recursive proof systems and resource-constrained environments.
